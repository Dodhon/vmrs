final report should acquaint the task organization, top management, and all other persons likely to be involved with the development, design, and implementation of the new system. The systems study is the point of departure for the implementation of VMRS and the design of the new system upon which it is based.

# 7. Approve the findings of the systems study from Step 6.

Upper management should approve the systems study in writing. Prior to approval, it may be necessary to brief upper management on various aspects of the report. Therefore, it's a good idea to prepare an executive summary of the full report for such meetings.

#### 8. Develop a VMRS implementation plan.

The implementation plan provides orderly structure to the implementation process, based on realistic time schedules, financial and personnel resources. The plan should specify assignments, costs, responsibilities and timetables for each step of the implementation process. If necessary, obtain upper management approval for the plan prior to implementation.

## 9. Design the new fleet management system around VMRS.

In designing a new equipment and maintenance information system, six basic questions must be answered initially:

- Will this be a computer- or manual-based information system?
- If computer-based, what computer will be used to process the data, both now and in the foreseeable future?
- What elements of existing systems, if any, will be preserved in the new system?
- If maintenance work is now performed at one location, can this work be expected to be performed at additional locations in future?
- By what date must the new system be operational?
- Are suitable computer applications available for purchase or lease?

When these questions are answered, the design process can proceed.

VMRS can provide users with as much or as little data and information as is desired. Because

various users will need varying amounts of detail, it is important to meet with those individuals who will ultimately use the information to determine their specific needs. Experience to date has shown that the more knowledgeable VMRS users become about the system, the more they demand from it.

#### 10. Develop all necessary data processing system applications (i.e., maintenance management software or paper-based data entry system) either internally, or through an outside supplier.

This step — and that of developing manual support procedures — should occur at the same time, if possible. The potential relationship between these two steps may point out systems and/or support requirements unforeseen in earlier states of the project.

In program design, modular programming is recommended to facilitate future development within the system, as well as in the overall systems relationship to other systems. Do preserve the ability to use all the features of VMRS in the system, even though initially their usage may not be required or envisioned. Also provide room for records of maximum size for future systems growth.

Use open-ended code lists so new codes can be added later. And, program for nine-digit component codes, even though initial requirements may only be for three- or six-digit codes. (Refer to Code Key 33.) Finally, write the required applications as needed.

## 11. Develop/obtain VMRS training materials and support procedures.

The design of the systems paper flow should include all steps from initial data entry to final systems input. The responsibilities of specific position job descriptions for the data input, review and supervision and paper flow should be assigned at this step.

Backup or contingency plans should be made in the event that normal support procedures are disrupted, such as using truck mail to substitute for e-mail, or postal service. Perform a critical analysis of the manual support procedures to determine possible causes and effects of such disruptions, and alternate means of functioning.

#### 12. Train all necessary personnel.

Personnel can make or break even the most carefully designed system. If the project has the full support of the highest levels of management, personnel resistance to change can be more readily overcome.

All participants should receive appropriate training in VMRS and the overall management system, as it directly impacts their work. Those individuals directly responsible for data input or subsequent review or verification of basic data (mechanics, supervisors, parts specialists, etc.) must appreciate how their performance is critical to the success of the entire system.

The training phase offers an excellent opportunity to gather both test data and historical data. Early introduction of the VMRS Repair Order Form (*Form 6*) will capture basic data in its new format, and enable test data to be live data. Furthermore, this live data can be used in any existing system as well as stored for subsequent entry into the new system. Thus, when the new system is implemented, a meaningful file of relevant past data will be available in VMRS format for initial output reports.

#### 13. "Beta" test the VMRS system.

Good data samples should be used to beta test the system, and can best be obtained by following the procedures outlined previously.

Test results should be analyzed to uncover programming or input errors, as well as errors in output reports. If a present maintenance information system exists, it may be possible to compare and validate some if the test data with the live data of the current system. Furthermore, the two systems can be run on a parallel basis. Problems uncovered should be shared with those concerned, and resolved. Subsequently, programming changes can be made.

Test/sample reports should be provided to all users for review, comments, recommendations, and approval. Finally, changes should be made as necessary.

#### 14. Document VMRS system performance.

The four key elements to this step are:

- prepare systems operations flow charts and instructions.
- prepare data entry instructions.
- document the system.
- document applications programming.

# 15. Write or modify training materials and procedures as necessary.

This step creates institutional memory of how the system functions. As personnel change, these materials will increase in value. It is essential that formal procedural references be available to train new and current personnel. Development and maintenance of the systems and procedures manual should be assigned to the one manager who has the fullest understanding of the system at the operating level.

#### 16. Fully implement VMRS.

The actual implementation of a VMRS-based maintenance management system can be accomplished in several ways. If an existing system has been in operation, the two systems can be run parallel, beyond the test phase, so as to thoroughly familiarize all persons with all phases of the new system.

If, on the other hand, management believes the system can be implemented without further trial and training, a set date can be established to terminate the old system and begin the new system.

If multiple shop locations are involved, it may be a good idea to phase in the new system at one or several sites at a time. This permits onsite supervision by management to ensure implementation is off to a good start.

It may be a good idea to convert historical data to the new system, if justifiable.

Closely monitor the new system and evaluate its output and the relationship it has with other systems. Fine tuning is easier to accomplish earlier, rather than later. Additionally, solicit feedback from all relevant parties—from the shop floor to the front office—so necessary adjustments can be made as quickly as possible.

# 17. Review, evaluate, enhance and expand VMRS usage, either continually or periodically.

All management information systems should undergo periodic review or audit to determine whether or not they are functioning efficiently. For example, the equipment that supports the system may become less efficient and/or more costly as technology improves.

New management information requirements may overlook the fact that the basic information sought may be derived from data already being recorded, or available to be recorded in the existing system. Similarly, new information requirements may be met with data available for processing from both the maintenance and other information systems.

Enhancements should be treated as if one were establishing a new system. The same considerations should be applied even to "one-time only" projects. Finally, the continuing process of fine-tuning should seek as its goal the best possible information at the least expense.

#### Conclusion

Equipment and maintenance reporting is not a speciality. It is a major component of any fleet's data reporting and analysis effort. As such, maintenance reporting should be a thoroughly integrated component of both the basic fleet management information system and its subsidiary systems.

# 4. Preparing Effective Equipment Maintenance Management Reports

#### Introduction

Preparing meaningful equipment maintenance management reports is a task that continually challenges even the best fleet managers and information specialists. The variety of such reports is almost endless, and basic maintenance data is processed and presented in almost as many formats as there are reports and equipment users.

Reports are in no way a substitute for the knowledge and judgement of a professional equipment manager. The manager and information systems specialist should work together to define what information is needed and how it can be most effectively presented.

One reason for such a variety of maintenance reports is the very variety of maintenance variables that can be identified, measured, and compared. These variables include:

- individual equipment units
- locations of maintenance activities
- causes of equipment failures
- equipment systems
- equipment subsystems
- individual equipment components.

Each of these factors have their own separate characteristics and reliability, adding to the complexity of maintenance reports.

VMRS provides a system of equipment maintenance recordkeeping that enables managers to assemble information needed to plan and control maintenance functions. VMRS provides data element definitions and codes, as well as the basic forms for recording equipment identification and maintenance data. Furthermore, the codes provide the "handles" by which appropriate data items can be selected and processed in the data processing system to prepare useful management information reports. The format and content of management reports should reflect the needs and preferences of the managers who use them. Standardized reports that a rigidly structured and do not take into consideration the nature of the organization, the equipment and its operation are usually of little use.

There are seven key dimensions to management reports: content, detail, frame of reference, aids to analysis, readability, schedule, and economy. All reports should be evaluated for these attributes.

This section examines some equipment maintenance management reports, illustrating how various users have approached maintenance management reporting, the advantages and disadvantages of various approaches, and how the analysis of those approaches may provide guidelines for equipment maintenance reporting. The examples are not intended as specific recommendations of formatting and reporting, and should not be used for cost or other reference purposes.

**5.** *Maintenance Facility Summary Analysis* **Report 4-7** does not identify pieces of equipment which have been worked on. Rather, it presents a number of analyses, based on VMRS coding, of maintenance work which has been performed at a particular location. As noted in **Report 4-3**, controlling maintenance cost involves measuring the performance of the maintenance facility as well as that of the equipment unit or group. This report is actually four different reports segregated by VMRS system, including reason for repair, repair class, repair site, and work accomplished, as well as a parts and labor breakdown.

This report, like **Report 4-1**, contains information for the current period only. **Report 4-7** might be used by the manager of the repair facility to analyze the performance by that facility. A manager with overall responsibility could use such reports from all facilities for purposes of analysis by comparison.

**Report 4-7** is a monthly report. A similar report can be easily generated for the entire year. Such a report offers easy comparison of monthly figures, allowing the equipment manager to recognize the effects of winter weather or other unusual monthly variations. It also helps spot trends in maintenance costs and occurrences.

Trend reports may be based on time or mileage. Such reports provide information which assists management in making decisions relating to overhaul scheduling, major component replacement, and equipment retention.

**Report 4-8** is an example of a computer-generated trend analysis report that is intended to help equipment managers quickly review percentage and average calculations based on VMRS data. Each line of the report contains the values by month of the measurements described in the lefthand column. Each column contains those values for a given month, except for the last column which contains an average monthly value.

This report is possible thanks to computer data processing. Increased computing power has made such reports available to many fleet managers. However, to obtain the maximum value of a report like this, the report user should be an experienced professional in the field of equipment maintenance management with the time and the patience for in-depth analysis.

6. Maintenance Facility Rework Analysis Report 4-9 is an example of a report of a particular category of work performed at a maintenance facility. In this case, rework or "comebacks" are reported. Using VMRS coding, similar reports could be prepared for road calls, driver complaints, or other categories of maintenance causes and effects. A report of this type may be more readily justified on an on-call basis when a particular situation requires the close attention of management.

#### 7. Warranty Claims Analysis

**Report 4-10** is an example of a warranty claims analysis report. This report allows the user to view all the warranty claims that were generated by a specific location or region. The user has the ability to specify the date range for which this report can be generated. The report could also be sorted by warranty status. For example, the user could generate a report listing all warranty claims generated by Region 001, for the month of January 2005, that have a status of RA—claim received and accepted. Additionally, the user could specify a vendor for which the report is to be generated.

#### 8. Parts Usage Analysis

**Report 4-11** is an example of a parts usage report, offering a historical usage of a part or a series of parts. The report is sorted by VMRS component codes (Code Key 33). Similar reports could be generated for parts without usage (parts not used for a given period of time) or parts inventory.

**Report 4-12** is an example of a parts inventory summary report, sorted by VMRS component code (Code Key 33).

#### Conclusions

This section offered examples of general, equipment - or component-oriented maintenance reports which are in common use. The reports require that the records of parts and labor applied to equipment maintenance be coded to identify the equipment components affected. Such reports support analyses of component life expectancies and causes and costs of component failure. The information is valuable in making component specification and replacement decisions. Other reports support analyses of the effect of time and use on maintenance costs and indicate some of the mathematical approaches to maintenance reporting.

Preparation of such reports requires levels of data coding and processing which should be accessible by nearly all equipment users, regardless of fleet size. The information presented is basic. Report users will probably seek additional information to further define the nature of present problems requiring management action, the trends which may indicate such problems are developing, and the corrective action needed.

Because of the effort required to record and code information — as well as the complexities of presenting the information in a readily useful form — management may be somewhat reluctant to implement a comprehensive maintenance management information system. However, the choice is fundamentally one of management by intuition, rather than informed analysis and decision. As fleet operations grow ever larger and more complex, the worth of a maintenance management information system — such as VMRS<sup>™</sup> — becomes more obvious.

# 5. Integrating Maintenance, Fuel and Oil Information

Fleet managers responsible for equipment costs must have accurate cost information for all of their equipment. This information is crucial to the development of future specifications, the selection of manufacturers and suppliers, and equipment retention decisions.

In order to analyze the cost of equipment after it has been placed in service, the following data must be obtained:

- Labor cost by equipment system
- Parts cost by equipment system
- Fuel consumption per mile
- Oil consumption per mile

To efficiently produce this information in a usable format requires the integration of maintenance data and fuel/oil data by piece of equipment.

The basic maintenance, fuel and oil data necessary for successful integration include:

#### Fuel and Oil

- a. Equipment Fleet Identification Number
- b. Date fuel or oil added
- c. Mileage at (b.)
- d. Location where fuel or oil added
- e. Quantity added
- f. Cost of fuel or oil added

#### Maintenance

- a. Equipment Fleet Identification Number
- b. Date maintenance performed
- c. Mileage at (b.)
- d. Location where work performed
- e. Parts added
- f. Parts cost
- g. Labor hours
- h. Labor cost

The common data element contained in these two data sets — the Equipment Fleet Identification Number — permits the development of reports containing data elements from each file.

#### **Data Collection and Processing**

#### 1. Fuel and Oil

Data on fuel and oil added (i.e., engine oil added between scheduled drain periods) may be obtained from the fleet's internal fuel control system, receipts for outside purchases, or maintenance activities.

VMRS<sup>™</sup> component codes (Code Key 33) have been assigned to identify equipment fluids and gases. These VMRS codes permit fuel use data recording by equipment. Their use facilitates the integration of maintenance, fuel and oil data for consolidated management information reports. The complete list of such codes for equipment fluids and gases appears within Code Key 33, beginning with System/Assembly level code 053-999. For example, the codes for fuel and engine oil are:

- 053-999-013—Fuel, Diesel Engine
- 053-999-014—Fuel, Gasoline Engine
- 053-999-015—Fuel, Propane Engine
- 053-999-016—Oil, Engine

#### 2. Maintenance

Maintenance data is collected through the fleet's maintenance reporting system.

#### 3. Data Collection

Thus, data on engine oil consumed is captured at the source—in this case, the maintenance facility. Other primary points for capturing such data would be service or inspection lanes, fuel facilities, and locations of outside fuel and oil purchases.

#### 4. Reports

To be useful, output reports must contain meaningful information in a convenient format. Recording detailed information is needed to identify specific problems. However, it is often unnecessary to report such detail to satisfy management information requirements. Fuel and oil data, as well as cost-per-mile data, lend themselves to both summary and exception reporting. Management can establish parameters for exception reports which can be developed to identify only those pieces of equipment falling above or below established parameters. By eliminating the vast amount of "normal" information, reports are reduced to manageable proportions so as to highlight those "abnormal" items worthy of management's attention.
